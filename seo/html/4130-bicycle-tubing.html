Bicycle Frame Tube Selection

Bicycle frame tube selection balances OD (which governs stiffness for a given wall), wall thickness (which governs weight and strength), and the tube geometry of each member. Main triangle tubes (top tube, down tube, seat tube) handle primarily bending and compression loads. Chainstays and seatstays primarily see bending and axial loads from pedalling forces. Different tubes in the same frame are often different sizes and wall thicknesses for optimal weight efficiency.

Butted Tubing

High-quality 4130 bicycle frame tubing is often 'butted' — the tube wall is thicker at the ends (where the maximum stress occurs at the tube joint) and thinner in the middle (where lower stress allows weight reduction). Double-butted tubing has thick walls at both ends and thin in the middle. Triple-butted has three different wall thicknesses. Butted tubing is a specialist product distinct from standard CDS mechanical tubing with uniform wall.

For frame builders using standard CDS 4130 mechanical tubing, the uniform-wall product is used with good results, particularly for production work or where butted tube lead times and minimums are impractical.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is standard ASTM A519 CDS 4130 acceptable for bicycle frame construction?

Yes. Many quality steel frames are built from standard uniform-wall CDS 4130 tube. The butted frame tubing available from specialist bicycle tube suppliers (e.g., Reynolds, Columbus) provides weight savings but is a specialist product. For prototypes, custom builds, or builders who cannot access minimum quantities of butted tube, standard CDS 4130 is a legitimate alternative.

What OD is typically used for a bicycle main triangle?

Typical road and mountain bike frame tubes use ODs in the 28mm–38mm range (approximately 1.1"–1.5"). Exact sizing depends on frame design, rider load, and the builder's structural analysis. This is general guidance — verify with your frame design calculations.
